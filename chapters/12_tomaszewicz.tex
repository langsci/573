\abstract{I define the semantics of four exclusive adverbials in Polish -- \textit{tylko}, \textit{zaledwie}, \textit{dopiero}, and \textit{aż} -- as composed of three core components: an assertion of exclusivity, a factive presupposition and a scalar presupposition. By minimally adjusting parameters such as the direction and dimension of the scale and the size of the associated focus, different flavors of exclusivity emerge. These parameter settings give rise to temporal readings (\textit{dopiero}), approximative and counterfactual readings (\textit{zaledwie}), and even additive-like readings (\textit{aż}).

\keywords{\textit{only}, \textit{even}, exclusivity, scalarity, assertion, presupposition, focus association}

\section{Introduction}

This chapter presents the behavior of four exclusive adverbials in Polish, \textit{tylko}, \textit{zaledwie}, \textit{dopiero} and \textit{aż}, and shows how they can be defined as a family: Each lexical entry includes a common core (assertion of exclusivity, presupposition of scalarity) with minimal differences in specific parameters (the direction and the dimension of the scale, the size of the associated focus).\footnote{The glosses of the Polish particles in the examples reflect the parallels with the English counterparts. In \sectref{sec:tom:4}, we will see that \textit{tylko} is similar to \textit{only} and \textit{zaledwie} to \textit{merely}. \textit{Dopiero} has a German counterpart, \textit{erst}, which has been named \textsc{temporal} \textit{only}. \textit{Aż} on my view is a scalar opposite of \textit{merely}, so it has no approximate translation and is glossed here as \textsc{aż}.} We will start with a context where all four adverbs have a similar effect on the meaning and then use a battery of tests to identify the individual meaning components.

All of the adverbs contribute to the sentence in \REF{ex:tom:1} the additional meaning that the 30\textsuperscript{th} place is a weak result, i.e. the team did not perform any better than that.

\ea\label{ex:tom:1} 
\gll Zespół był     słaby  i         zajął         \minsp{\{} tylko / zaledwie / dopiero / aż\}   30   miejsce.\\
team    was  poor    and    achieved  {} only {} merely {} only\textsubscript{\textsc{temp}} {} \textsc{aż}     30\textsuperscript{th}  place\\
\glt `The team performed poorly and earned only 30\textsuperscript{th} place.'
\z

\noindent %The 
English \textit{only} has exactly this meaning `(at least) $X$ and nothing more', with `nothing more' excluding all contextually ranked higher alternatives. Is it only a coincidence that in this particular context the four adverbials seem to mean `only'? Could the sentence rather be saying that ‘The team was ranked as low as the 30\textsuperscript{th} place’? This meaning is similar but it does not contain the exclusive component `nothing more'. I argue that the four adverbial particles should all be classified as exclusives, i.e., they all share the exclusive meaning component, and that this primary component is at-issue (\citealt{Potts2005}, \citealt{beaverclark08}, \citealt{Simons.etal2011}, \citealt{roberts11}). Each one of them, however, has its own flavor so that in most contexts they are not interchangeable as they are in \REF{ex:tom:1}. I identify the differences in their use that make them similar only under certain conditions. It turns out that Polish does not have one adverbial that can contribute the exclusive meaning in all contexts, the way %the 
English \textit{only} can. Instead, each of the items places particular restrictions on the dimension of scale of the excluded alternatives. Furthermore, the direction of exclusion differs: alternatives both higher and lower on the scale can be excluded \citep{tomaszewicz12,tomaszewicz13,tomaszewicz13a}. I propose lexical entries for each exclusive such that they all include a common core with minimal differences in specific parameters.

\section{Exclusivity and scalarity}
\label{sec:tom:2}

The adverb \textit{only} contributes the meaning of exclusion: in contrast to \REF{ex:tom:2}, the sentence in \REF{ex:tom:3} states that Maria met Jan and she did not meet anyone else. 

\ea\label{ex:tom:2}
Maria met Jan.
\z 

\ea\label{ex:tom:3} 
Maria met only Jan.
\z

\noindent In some contexts \textit{only} additionally contributes scalarity by implying a ranking among the excluded alternatives. Consider how  in \REF{ex:tom:4} we understand that the 30th place is not a good result, even though we do not know how many places were assigned in total. In \REF{ex:tom:5} it is implied that 9 PM is late, and in \REF{ex:tom:6}--\REF{ex:tom:7} that the manager is lower on the scale than some contextually relevant alternative.

\ea\label{ex:tom:4}
Jan got only the 30th place.
\z

\ea\label{ex:tom:5}
Jan arrived only at 9 PM.
\z

\ea\label{ex:tom:6}
Jan is only/merely a manager.
\z

\ea\label{ex:tom:7}
Maria talked only/merely to the manager.
\z

\noindent In the example in \REF{ex:tom:4} it is obvious that you can be ranked at a single place only, so the exclusive meaning should be paraphrased as `Jan got no better place than the 30th' rather than `Jan got the 30th place and no other place' in contrast to the paraphrase of the sentence in \REF{ex:tom:3}. These two readings of \textit{only} have been called \textsc{scalar} and \textsc{quantificational} (\citealt{bonomicasalegno93}, \citealt{krifka93}, \citealt{klinedinst05}, \citealt{riester06}, \citealt{beaverclark08}, \citealt{zeevat09}, among others). The scalar reading of the sentence in \REF{ex:tom:5} is roughly equivalent to `Jan arrived no sooner than 9 PM' because the most plausible scenario is that Jan arrived once, as opposed to `Jan arrived at 9 PM and at no other time', which would be the quantificational reading. In contrast to \REF{ex:tom:4}, in \REF{ex:tom:6} Jan is a manager in addition to being other obvious things such as human, terrestrial, mortal… These properties are not ordered on the scale with respect to the property of being a manager, they are not contextually relevant. Thus, in \REF{ex:tom:6}, \textit{only} evokes a contextually relevant scale and places ‘manager’ low on that scale, as illustrated in \REF{ex:tom:8}. The example in \REF{ex:tom:7} also shows that the scale of exclusion involves only relevant alternatives, i.e., the people ranked in terms of importance in \REF{ex:tom:8}, because \REF{ex:tom:7} is true even if Maria talked to the manager’s secretary (assuming that this conversation was irrelevant to the topic of her conversation with the manager). Note that in English \textit{merely} is dedicated to scalar readings, \REF{ex:tom:6}--\REF{ex:tom:7} \citep{coppockbeaver11, coppockbeaver14}.

\ea\label{ex:tom:8}
Scale for \REF{ex:tom:6}--\REF{ex:tom:7}:\\
`Jan is the president.'\\
`Jan is a vice president.'\\
...\\
`Jan is a senior manager.'\\
`Jan is a manager.'\\
`Jan is an assistant manager.'\\
...\\
`Jan is an employee.'
\z 

\noindent The Polish exclusives in the example in \REF{ex:tom:1}, \textit{tylko}, \textit{zaledwie}, \textit{dopiero} and \textit{aż}, are all compatible with the scalar readings, but now the examples in \REF{ex:tom:9}--\REF{ex:tom:12} will show that Polish does not have one adverbial that can contribute the exclusive meaning in all contexts like %the 
English \textit{only} can.\footnote{All non-English examples in this chapter are from Polish, unless indicated otherwise.} The quantificational reading requires \textit{tylko}, as shown in the sentence in \REF{ex:tom:9} which is the counterpart of the sentence we saw in \REF{ex:tom:3}. With temporal scales, on the other hand, \textit{tylko} cannot be used, \REF{ex:tom:10}, unlike \textit{only} in \REF{ex:tom:5}. With the scale of important people, \REF{ex:tom:11}--\REF{ex:tom:12}, \textit{tylko}, \textit{zaledwie} and \textit{dopiero} are possible, though here the manager is low on the scale, just like in \REF{ex:tom:6}--\REF{ex:tom:7} with \textit{only/merely}.

\ea\label{ex:tom:9}
\gll Maria   spotkała \minsp{\{} tylko / \minsp{\#} zaledwie / \minsp{\#} dopiero / \minsp{\#} aż\}   Jana.\\
Maria  met {} only {} {} merely {} {}  only\textsubscript{\textsc{temp}} {} {} \textsc{aż}            Jan\\
\glt ‘Maria met only Jan and nobody else.’
\z

\ea\label{ex:tom:10}
\gll Jan   przyjechał  \minsp{\{} dopiero / aż / \minsp{\#} tylko / \minsp{?} zaledwie\}   o   9-tej.\\
Jan arrived {} only\textsubscript{\textsc{temp}} {} \textsc{aż} {} {} only {} {} merely at 9\\
\glt ‘Jan arrived only at 9 PM and no sooner.’
\z 

\ea\label{ex:tom:11}
\gll Jan jest \minsp{\{} tylko / zaledwie / dopiero /  \minsp{\#} aż\}     menedżerem.\\
Jan is {} only {} merely {}  only\textsubscript{\textsc{temp}} {} {} \textsc{aż} manager\\
\glt ‘Jan is only/merely a manager (so far) and nobody more important.’
\z 

\ea\label{ex:tom:12}
\gll Maria rozmawiała \minsp{\{} tylko / zaledwie / dopiero / \minsp{\#} aż\} z menedżerem.\\
Maria talked {} only {} merely {} only\textsubscript{\textsc{temp}} {} {} \textsc{aż} with manager\\
\glt ‘Maria only/merely talked to the manager (so far) and nobody more important.’
\z 

\noindent Crucially, the scalar readings of \REF{ex:tom:11}--\REF{ex:tom:12} “flip” when \textit{aż}  is used: In \REF{ex:tom:13} the manager is interpreted to be high on the scale of importance. The exclusive \textit{aż} results in a reading that is similar to the effect of the addition of \textit{even} (Polish \textit{nawet}). In the sentence with \textit{nawet} in \REF{ex:tom:14}, similarly to \REF{ex:tom:13}, the manager is implied to be a noteworthy person for Maria to talk to. However, \textit{nawet} is not compatible with the continuation that Maria did not talk to anybody else, \REF{ex:tom:14}, in contrast to \textit{aż} in \REF{ex:tom:15}. This is because \textit{nawet}/\textit{even} is an additive scalar particle (\citealt{horn69}, \citealt{karttunenpeters79}, \citealt{rooth85,rooth92}, a.o.), whereas \textit{aż} is an exclusive as I argued in \citet{tomaszewicz12,tomaszewicz13,tomaszewicz13a}.

\ea\label{ex:tom:13}
\gll Maria   rozmawiała   \minsp{\{\#} tylko / \minsp{\#} zaledwie / \minsp{\#} dopiero / aż\}   z       menedżerem.\\
Maria talked {} only {} {} merely {} {} only\textsubscript{\textsc{temp}} {} \textsc{aż} with  manager\\
\glt ‘Maria talked to somebody so important as the manager.’
\z 

\ea\label{ex:tom:14}
\gll Maria rozmawiała nawet z menedżerem, \minsp{\#} ale nie rozmawiała z nikim innym.\\
Maria talked even with  manager {} but not talked with nobody else\\
\glt `Maria even talked to the manager, \textit{[Incongruous:]} but she did not talk to anybody else.’
\z 

\ea\label{ex:tom:15}
\gll Maria rozmawiała aż z menedżerem, ale nie rozmawiała z nikim innym. \\
Maria talked \textsc{aż} with  manager but not talked with  nobody else\\
\glt `Maria talked to somebody so important as the manager, but she did not talk to anybody else.’
\z 


\noindent I proposed in \citet{tomaszewicz12,tomaszewicz13,tomaszewicz13a} that \textit{aż} is the scalar opposite of \textit{merely}/\textit{zaledwie}: It asserts exclusion just like \textit{only}/\textit{merely}, evokes scales like \textit{merely}, but it places the mentioned alternative high on the scale. In this chapter, I provide tests to specify the meaning components for each of the exclusives shown in \REF{ex:tom:1}, demonstrating that the semantics contains a common core predicting the existence of scalar opposites like \textit{aż} cross-linguistically, even though such a lexical item is unavailable in, for instance, English.

Our starting point is the basic meaning contribution of \textit{only}/\textit{merely}, which involves, on the one hand, exclusivity \REF{ex:tom:16a}--\REF{ex:tom:16b} and, on the other hand, the prejacent, which is the meaning of the sentence without \textit{only}/\textit{merely} \REF{ex:tom:16c} (\citealt{klinedinst05}, \citealt{roberts06}, \citealt{vanrooijschulz07}, \citealt{beaverclark08}, a.o.).\footnote{This use of the term \textsc{prejacent} was introduced by \citet{horn96}; however, \citet{roberts06} observes that often with \textit{only} the prejacent does not correspond to any overt syntactic constituent. \citet{vonfintelheim02} use the term for the constituent that is the sister of the operator at the level of logical syntax. \citet{roberts06} addresses the debate on the status of the prejacent with \textit{only} arguing that it is “closer to a presupposition than to an entailment or a conversational implicature”. \citet{crnic12} argues that the mixed behavior (prejacent presupposition vs. existential presupposition) results from two separate meaning components at LF with different scope.}

\ea\label{ex:tom:16}
Maria only talked to the manager.\smallskip\\
\textit{Exclusive/negative contribution}:
\ea\label{ex:tom:16a}   
`Maria did not talk to anybody else than the manager.'\\\hfill \textsc{quantificational reading}
\ex\label{ex:tom:16b}  
`Maria did not talk to anybody more noteworthy than a manager.'\\\hfill \textsc{scalar reading}
\z
\textit{Positive contribution}:
\begin{xlist}
\exi{c.}{`Maria talked to (at least) the manager.'}\hfill \textsc{prejacent}\label{ex:tom:16c}

\end{xlist} 
\z 

\noindent The quantificational reading in \REF{ex:tom:16a} can be seen as an instance of exclusion along a logical/entailment scale (\citealt{bonomicasalegno93}, \citealt{vanrooij02}, \citealt{beaver04}, \citealt{klinedinst05}, \citealt{riester06}, a.o.). The dimension of the scale is set relevant to the context, and where no such pragmatic ordering is readily available (such as the ranking of people in terms of importance), the logical scale is the default (e.g., all other people are excluded).

In the following sections I present a battery of tests to demonstrate that with all four Polish adverbial particles, \textit{tylko}, \textit{zaledwie}, \textit{dopiero} and \textit{aż}, exclusivity is asserted, the prejacent is presupposed, and scalarity, i.e., the `low/high on the scale' reading, is presupposed. In addition to the exclusive assertion and the prejacent presupposition, it will be shown that scalarity comes from focus association,  so that the focal presupposition supplies the set of alternatives for the ranking.

\section{Focus association}
\label{sec:tom:3}

\textit{Only} belongs to so-called \textsc{focus-sensitive} adverbs because its surface position interacts with the placement of focus. The semantic effects of focus on the interpretation of \textit{only} go hand in hand with syntactic/phonological effects, i.e., the way \textit{only} modifies the meaning of the sentence depends on which constituent is focused, although the syntactic associate needs not always be marked by easily perceptible prosodic prominence. In Polish focus is marked prosodically, with pitch and intensity as the main acoustic correlates \citep{hamlaoui+19}. The focused constituent is called the \textsc{syntactic associator}. It can be a constituent that is immediately modified by \textit{only}, as we have seen above and as shown below in \REF{ex:tom:17}: `only for a loan'. 

\ea\label{ex:tom:17}
\gll Hanka   poprosiła   tylko [\textsubscript{PP} o       [\textsubscript{DP} pożyczkę]],   i       o     nic           więcej / innego.\\  
Hanka asked only {} for {} loan and for nothing more {} else  \\
\glt `Hanka only asked for a loan and nothing more/else.’
\z 

\noindent But the associator can also be more distant, as can be seen in \REF{ex:tom:18}. The different continuations in \REF{ex:tom:18a}--\REF{ex:tom:18d} result from different focus association configurations (associated with different prosodic patterns). Here \textit{tylko} precedes the verb but it can associate with the object DP so that the sentence has the exact same interpretation as in \REF{ex:tom:17}. It can also associate with the verb itself, as shown by the compatibility with the continuation in \REF{ex:tom:18b}, and it can associate with the whole VP, as evidenced by the continuation in \REF{ex:tom:18c}. In contrast to English, \textit{tylko} in the preverbal position can also associate with the subject DP, \REF{ex:tom:18d}. When \textit{tylko} precedes the subject it can, unsurprisingly, associate with it, \REF{ex:tom:19a}, but it can also associate with the whole sentence, \REF{ex:tom:19b}, which, again, is not possible in English.

\ea\label{ex:tom:18}
\gll {}[\textsubscript{DP} Hanka]   tylko [\textsubscript{VP} [\textsubscript{V} poprosiła]   o       [\textsubscript{DP} pożyczkę]],    \\
{} Hanka only {} {} asked for {} loan \\  
\ea\label{ex:tom:18a} 
\gll  … i o nic innego nie poprosiła.\\    
{} and for nothing else not asked\\\hfill \textsc{object} \textsc{DP} \textsc{focus} 
\glt `Hanka asked only for a loan and did not ask for anything else.’ \\
\ex\label{ex:tom:18b}
\gll … a nie zażądała.\\
{} and not demanded\\\hfill \textsc{V} \textsc{focus}
\glt `Hanka only asked for a loan and did not demand it.’
\ex\label{ex:tom:18c}
\gll … i nic innego nie zrobiła.\\
{} and nothing else not did\\\hfill \textsc{VP} \textsc{focus}
\glt `Hanka only asked for a loan and did not do anything else.’
\ex\label{ex:tom:18d}      
\gll … i nikt inny nie prosił.\\
 {} and nobody else not asked\\\hfill \textsc{subject DP focus}
\glt `Only Hanka asked for a loan and nobody else did.’
\z 
\z 

\ea\label{ex:tom:19}
\gll \minsp{(} To) tylko [\textsubscript{S} [\textsubscript{DP} Hanka] poprosiła o pożyczkę],\\    
{} \textsc{top} only {} {} Hanka asked for loan\\      
\ea\label{ex:tom:19a}
\gll … i nikt inny nie prosił.\\
{} and nobody else not asked\\\hfill \textsc{subject} \textsc{DP} \textsc{focus}
\glt `Hanka only asked for a loan and nobody else did.’ 
\ex\label{ex:tom:19b} 
\gll … i nic innego się nie stało.\\                
{} and nothing else self not happened\\\hfill \textsc{sentence} \textsc{focus}
\glt `It is only that Hanka asked for a loan and nothing else happened.’
\z 
\z 

\noindent The associate determines the implicit comparison with alternatives of the same type. So when \textit{tylko} in the sentence-initial position associates with the whole clause (sentence focus in \REF{ex:tom:19b}), what is excluded are other things that could have happened that are ranked higher on the scale of importance. A typical context for \REF{ex:tom:19b} is a question ‘Did anything happen?’ and an answer such as ‘No worries. It is just …’. 

In \REF{ex:tom:20} I provide an example from a Polish comedy TV series ``Alternatywy 4'' (episode 9), from a scene where what appears to be a woman but is in fact a robot, jumps down from a balcony, breaks her neck severing the head, then stands up and picks up the head. The people witnessing this are screaming in shock and a man is trying to calm them down saying that nothing out of the ordinary is happening.

\ea\label{ex:tom:20}
\gll To nic       takiego, to nic         takiego! To tylko ten psychiczny dostał szału!\\
 it   nothing such       it nothing such       it   only     this lunatic       got     amok\\
\glt `It is nothing, it is nothing! It is just that lunatic going crazy!’
\z 

\noindent I am pointing out the differences between Polish and English because the syntax (and prosody) of focus association may vary considerably between languages. The extra options available in Polish are not limited to Slavic languages but are also possible in German (\citealt{bueringhartmann01}, \citealt{steindl13,steindl17}). In \REF{ex:tom:21} German \textit{nur} `only' associates with the subject DP that precedes it, while in \REF{ex:tom:22} \textit{nur} is in the sentence-initial position and can associate either with the subject or the whole clause. In English, on the other hand, the associates of \textit{only} must be in its surface c-command domain (\citealt{taglicht84}, \citealt{beaverclark08}, \citealt{toosarvandani10}, a.o.).

\ea\label{ex:tom:21}
\gll {}[\textsubscript{DP} {Juliane}]\textsubscript{F}   hat nur     die Blumen gegossen.\\                                           {} Juliane    has only    the flowers watered \\   
\glt `It was only Juliane that watered the flowers.’\hfill \citep[German;][]{steindl17}
\z 

\ea\label{ex:tom:22}
\gll Nur [\textsubscript{S} [\textsubscript{DP} {Juliane}]\textsubscript{F$_a$} spielt Klavier]\textsubscript{F$_b$}.\\                           
only {} {} Juliane plays piano\\
\ea `Only Juliane is playing the piano.’ \hfill \textsc{subject DP focus}
\ex `Nothing is going on except for Juliane playing the piano.’ \\\hfill \textsc{sentence focus} 
\z \hfill\citep[German;][]{steindl13}
\z 

\noindent Of course, the different focus association patterns are associated with different prosodic contours of the sentence; specifically, the location of pitch accent and nuclear stress identify the constituents in focus. Without going into details of phonology, syntactic evidence for obligatory focus association comes from clitics. Clitics and reduced pronouns force a wider domain reading (\REF{ex:tom:23b} vs. \REF{ex:tom:23a}), which shows that \textit{tylko} cannot associate with weak (unfocused) pronouns. In \REF{ex:tom:23b} \textit{tylko} has to associate with the adverbial or the verb, which can be independently focused (cf. \citealt{hoeksemazwarts91}, \citealt{beaverclark08} on focus association of \textit{only} in Dutch).

\ea\label{ex:tom:23}
\ea\label{ex:tom:23a}  
\gll {}[\textsubscript{Adv} Wieczorem] tylko widziałem [\textsubscript{DP} niego].\\ 
{} evening only saw {} him\\  
\ea `In the evening I saw him and nobody else.’
\ex `In the evening, and at no other time, I saw him.’
\z 
\ex\label{ex:tom:23b}  
\gll {}[\textsubscript{Adv} Wieczorem]   tylko [\textsubscript{VP} go [\textsubscript{V} widziałem]].\\
{} evening  only {} him {} saw\\
\ea `In the evening I saw him and did not e.g. hear him.’
\ex `In the evening, and at no other time, I saw him.’
\ex \textit{Unavailable:} ‘In the evening I saw him and nobody else.’
\z 
\z 
\z 

\noindent The syntactic tests above, the interaction between the size of the focused constituent and the syntactic position of \textit{tylko} \REF{ex:tom:18}--\REF{ex:tom:19}, and the inability of \textit{tylko} to associate with non-focused elements such as clitics \REF{ex:tom:23}, suggest that \textit{tylko} obligatorily associates with focus.~The same can be shown for other Polish exclusives, as demonstrated for \textit{aż} in \citet{tomaszewicz12,tomaszewicz13,tomaszewicz13a}.

\section{Different ways to exclude alternatives}
\label{sec:tom:4}

\subsection{\textit{tylko}}
\label{sec:tom:4.1}

\textit{Only} is standardly assumed to contribute both to the assertion and the presupposition of the sentence in which it occurs (\citealt{horn69}, \citealt{klinedinst05}, \citealt{roberts06,roberts11}, \citealt{vanrooijschulz07}, \citealt{beaverclark08}). The asserted meaning can be either scalar (the dedicated use of \textit{merely}) or quantificational. In \REF{ex:tom:11}--\REF{ex:tom:12} we saw that \textit{tylko} like \textit{only} can have both scalar and quantificational readings; \REF{ex:tom:12} is repeated below in \REF{ex:tom:24}, and the examples for the two types of scales are given in \REF{ex:tom:25}. In \REF{ex:tom:24} \textit{tylko} associates with the focus on the PP `with the manager'.

\ea\label{ex:tom:24}
\gll Maria rozmawiała tylko z menedżerem.\\
Maria talked only with manager\\
\glt `Maria only talked to the/a manager.’\\
%\textrm{⤳} 
\textsc{Scalar} \textsc{reading}: `Maria talked to nobody ranked higher than the/a manager.'\\
%\textrm{⤳} 
\textsc{quantificational} \textsc{reading}: `Maria talked to nobody other than the/a manager.'
\z

\noindent Quantificational \textit{only} asserts that no other alternative is true, e.g., Maria only talked to the manager and nobody else. As noted in the previous section, the alternatives are either seen as not ordered or as an instance of exclusion along a logical/entailment scale, such as a numeral scale, as shown in (\ref{ex:tom:25}b) (\citealt{vanrooij02}, \citealt{klinedinst05}, \citealt{riester06}, \citealt{coppockbeaver11}, a.o.). The dimension of the scale is set pragmatically, and in contexts where no pragmatic ordering is available, the logical scale is the default.

Using tests for presupposition, such as embedding under a question operator or negation (\citealt{chierchiamcconnellginet90}), we can identify three meaning components of \textit{only}, one asserted (exclusivity) and two presupposed components (the prejacent and the ‘low on the scale’ reading).\footnote{Note that there is extensive debate on the nature of the non-truth conditional, i.e., projective content. Many different subtypes have been discussed, including pragmatic and semantic presuppositions \citep{stalnaker73}, conversational and conventional implicature (\citealt{grice81}, \citealt{bach94}, \citealt{Potts2005,potts15}), F-implicature \citep{horn13}, proffered content \citep{roberts96} and several other types and their groupings (e.g., Classes A--D in \citealt{tonhauser+13}).} 
We will use these tests on the three meaning components of the sentence in \REF{ex:tom:24} identified in (\ref{ex:tom:26}a--c). The asserted component is the exclusion of all alternatives higher on the scale: the rank of the person that Maria talked to is no higher than a manager, \REF{ex:tom:26a}.\footnote{The alternative `Maria talked to nobody' is also excluded, which, as suggested by Radek Šimík, could be seen as lower, and not higher, on the scale. However, the logical/entailment scale on the quantificational reading is not one-dimensional but it is a mereological structure (\citealt{riester06}, \citealt{coppockbeaver11}).} \noindent Additionally, \textit{only/merely} contributes two presuppositions, \REF{ex:tom:26b}, \REF{ex:tom:26c}. First, it presupposes that the alternatives under consideration are at least as high on the scale as the prejacent (\citealt{klinedinst05}, \citealt{beaverclark08}). Other relevant ranks of the person that Maria talked to are at least as high as the manager, \REF{ex:tom:26b} (lower ranks are simply not relevant). The second presupposition is that the prejacent is low on the scale of alternative propositions, which I paraphrase as ‘is not significant’, \REF{ex:tom:26c} \citep{klinedinst05}.

%\textrm{⤳} 
\ex\label{ex:tom:26b}
Maria talked to at least a manager. \hfill \textsc{Presupposition}\\
%\textrm{⤳} 
\ex\label{ex:tom:26c}
Maria’s talking to a manager is not significant. \hfill \textsc{Presupposition}
\z

\noindent \citet{beaverclark08} and \citet{zeevat09} call the low-on-the-scale reading of \textit{only} \textsc{mirative}. As a mirative marker \textit{only} implies that the prejacent falls short of what is expected. This reading is easily detected with numerals, for instance, if you hear \textit{The driver is only 1 stop away}, you know that 1 stop is very close. However, the sentence \textit{The driver is only 15 stops away} also tells you that the driver is close -- contrary to what you would expect given the usual duration of 15 stops (so for this statement to be true these 15 stops must be very short). Without \textit{only}, the sentence \textit{The driver is 15 stops away} does not create any expectation whether the driver is going to reach you soon or not.

\noindent If the dimension of the scale is underspecified in the semantics of an exclusive adverb, we expect it to be easily manipulated by the context (the semantics of the focused constituent the adverb associates with, e.g. `February' above, and the pragmatics). The four Polish exclusives, however, appear to be more contextually restricted. Distance, temporal and numeral scales are all natural, inherent, dimensional scales, so the prediction should be that an exclusive that works with any kind of a scale is available in any language. This seems to be the case for \textit{only} in English:

\z

\noindent If %the 
Polish \textit{tylko} can act scalar with individuals ordered on a contextual scale (importance, etc.) why is it incompatible with a temporal scale? In the next sections we will see that \textit{zaledwie} is dedicated to pragmatic scales (just like \textit{merely}) and \textit{dopiero} to temporal scales. This means that \textit{dopiero} might be blocking \textit{tylko} (but not \textit{zaledwie}) with a temporal scale, and that \textit{zaledwie} does not block \textit{tylko} with contextual scales (which perhaps suggests an interplay between the conventional nature of a scale and frequency in language use given that \textit{zaledwie} is less frequent than \textit{tylko}).

In addition to its use as a scalar particle, \textit{aż} functions in Polish as a complementizer, equivalent to \textit{until} in English, \REF{ex:tom:74a}. As a complementizer, it is obligatorily sentence-initial, but as we saw in \sectref{sec:tom:3} focus associators can associate with foci of all sizes, including the whole sentence in Polish. I propose that the lexical semantics of \textit{aż} is the same when it is interpreted as \textit{until}, \REF{ex:tom:74a}--\REF{ex:tom:74b}, and when it is a scalar adverb, \REF{ex:tom:74c}, but the syntax determines whether the ordering of alternatives is on a temporal scale or a pragmatic scale.\footnote{\label{fn:tom:former4}I thank Radek Šimík for pointing out that the other exclusive particles cannot switch in a parallel way with respect to the syntax, suggesting that the semantics of \textit{aż} alone cannot be responsible for its function as a complementizer. This is an interesting topic for (a cross-linguistic) investigation.} I observe that when the main clause explicitly mentions duration, \textit{aż} can either work as \textit{until}, \REF{ex:tom:74b}, or as the scalar exclusive in which case it is preceded by the complementizer \textit{that}, \REF{ex:tom:74c}. In \REF{ex:tom:74c} \textit{aż} can either associate with the subject so that the alternatives are people who could tell Ann to stop singing, or with the whole clause so that alternatives are other less serious things that may happen, e.g., the whole auditioning committee laughing.

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:tom:5}

The \textsc{factive} presupposition is the positive meaning component that projects under negation, therefore it is not just the prejacent proposition, but an alternative proposition that is at least as strong on the relevant scale (I am following \citealt{klinedinst05} and \citealt{coppockbeaver14}, but see also \citealt{beaverclark08} and \citealt{zeevat09} for a mirative presupposition that the prejacent falls short of what is expected). This positive contribution is paraphrasable with `at least' as we first saw in the example in \REF{ex:tom:16}: `Maria only talked to the manager' implies that `Maria talked to (at least) the manager', (\ref{ex:tom:16}c). \citet{coppockbeaver14} point out that this component is easier to see on scalar readings, in examples such as \REF{ex:tom:coppockbeaver14a}, than on quantificational readings, as in \REF{ex:tom:coppockbeaver14b}.
%e.g., `This is not \{just/a mere\} down payment' clearly tells us that `This is at least a down payment' (their examples (8--9) on p. 374). Since my analysis follows the accounts where the quantificational and scalar readings are derived from the same lexical entry (\citealt{bonomicasalegno93}, \citealt{vanrooij02}, \citealt{beaver04}, \citealt{klinedinst05}, \citealt{riester06}, \citealt{beaverclark08}, \citealt{coppockbeaver10, coppockbeaver14}), the factive presupposition of \textit{tylko/zaledwie} in (\ref{ex:tom:75a}) allows for both readings. As mentioned in \sectref{sec:tom:2}, the dimension of the scale is set by the context and  quantificational readings arise with logical scales, whereas scalar readings result from pragmatic orderings of alternatives in \textit{C.} In impoverished contexts the logical scale is the default.

\citet{greenberg22}, building on \citet{guerzoni03}, proposes that the scalar presupposition of \textit{only} is ``superlative'' so that $p$ is the weakest alternative in the context of focus alternatives, $C$. She also discusses how a general notion of stronger alternatives, symbolized as ‘$>$’, suffices, given that $C$ is similar to domain restriction on quantifiers and thus contains only contextually relevant alternatives, but at the same time the focus structure is constrained by the Question Under Discussion that is salient in the context (\citealt{rooth92}, \citealt{roberts96}, \citealt{beaverclark08}, \citealt{coppockbeaver10, coppockbeaver14}). Following \citet{guerzoni03}, \citet{greenberg22} treats \textit{only} and \textit{even} as antonyms, with mirroring superlative presuppositions, but adds special constraints on $C$ with respect to the QUD in order to account for the mirror image felicity patterns with \textit{only} and \textit{even}. She suggests that the superlative formulation of the scalar presupposition can be used to capture the differences in the types of scales with different operators, which is a question for future research. In this chapter, I focus on the common core of the exclusives, therefore I simply adopt the idea of stronger/weaker alternatives.

In (\ref{ex:tom:75}) above, I proposed a single lexical entry for \textit{tylko/zaledwie} because \textit{zaledwie} does not block \textit{tylko} from pragmatic scales and its use with numeral scales seems to only be dispreferred, as was shown in example \REF{ex:tom:37} in \sectref{sec:tom:4.2}. However, the approximative and counterfactual uses of \textit{(za)ledwie} require an extra presupposition: the lexical entry for \textit{(za)ledwie}\textsubscript{\textsc{approx}} makes it salient that \textit{not p} could happen, (\ref{ex:tom:76c}). is a salient alternative. Given that $C$ is determined also by the QUD, if $\neg p$ %\textit{not} \textit{p} 
is salient, the context disambiguates between the lexical entries in \REF{ex:tom:75} and \REF{ex:tom:76}.

\noindent Since \textit{aż} may but does not have to have a temporal reading like \textit{dopiero}, I propose that the temporal interpretation is determined by pragmatics and syntax. Namely, \textsc{temporal} \textit{aż} functions as a complementizer in Polish so it precedes a clause and the focus association mechanism derives a set of alternative propositions which are ordered with respect to time. As noted in footnote \ref{fn:tom:former4} the semantics and syntax alone cannot be the reason why \textit{aż} can function as a complementizer but the other exclusive particles cannot. I propose that when \textit{aż} associates with the whole sentence, as in \REF{ex:tom:85c}, the set of focus alternatives is the set of answers to the QUD, and pragmatics determines that the temporal ordering is the most relevant. What possibly contributes to this interpretation is the fact that without context a sort of default ordering for multiple events is a temporal ordering. In other words, I assume that, unlike for \textit{dopiero}, for \textsc{temporal} \textit{aż} no extra presupposition is necessary in the lexical entry. The set $C$ in \REF{ex:tom:85e} evokes multiple events and times for the set of propositions derived from wide focus. So what ends up being excluded are alternative propositions describing different events that happened at different times. The scalar presupposition says that those times are ordered on a scale. \section{Family members in other Slavic languages}
\label{sec:tom:6}

In \sectref{sec:tom:3}, I discussed the different syntactic configurations for focus association and noted that not all of them may be available in every language; e.g., in English \textit{only} in the clause-initial position cannot associate with the whole clause as it is possible in Polish or German. Now that I identified the minimal semantic differences between the four exclusives in Polish, we can expect that those same parameters will have different settings cross-linguistically and not all of the options may be available or, conversely, there could be more options for variation than in Polish. 

For instance, the limits on scale dimension that are lacking with %the 
English \textit{only}, are also found with %the 
German \textit{erst} \citep{koenig91}, a temporal exclusive like \textit{dopiero}. Several other Slavic languages have a counterpart of \textit{aż}, \textit{až} in Czech, Slovak and Russian, and \textit{čak} in Bulgarian, Serbian and other South Slavic languages (\citealt{tomaszewicz13,tomaszewicz13a}), but they seem to have various requirements on the type of scale. While in Russian \textit{až} is highly expressive and its use is considered colloquial, at first sight, the same range of contexts I have considered here for Polish seems to be available except for the two scope options with negation (Katja Jasinskaja, p.c.). In Czech and Slovak, on the other hand, there seems to be a strong requirement for temporal or distance scales, specifically with DP focus. Thus \REF{ex:tom:90} in Czech does not have the reading that the manager is a noteworthy person for Maria to talk to, but instead a temporal scale needs to be accommodated, as indicated by the translation (Radek Šimík, p.c.)\footnote{There may be variation among the speakers. \noindent However, \textit{až} in Czech can also associate with a VP and evoke a pragmatic scale (\citealt{tomaszewicz13,tomaszewicz13a}). In \REF{ex:tom:92}
% \citep[from][]{capek91}
the alternatives are different things that could result from the boy’s surprise; there is no implication of a temporal ordering.

\ea\label{ex:tom:92}
\gll Chlapec v zeleném overalu se překvapením až [\textsubscript{VP} začervenal].\\
boy in green overalls self surprise \textsc{až} {} reddened\\% \citep{capek91}
\glt `The boy in a green overalls as much as blushed with surprise.’\\\hfill (Czech; K. Čapek: \textit{Od člověka k člověku III})
\z 

\noindent In Slovak \textit{až} can have temporal readings when modifying a DP with a temporal reading, such as ‘Friday’ in \REF{ex:tom:sl1a}--\REF{ex:tom:sl1b}, without the high-on-the-scale implication that is found in such cases in Polish, i.e., in both \REF{ex:tom:sl1a}--\REF{ex:tom:sl1b} the meaning is `until Friday' with the implication that Friday is included (`through Friday' in American English). Compare how the Polish counterpart of \REF{ex:tom:sl1a} contains a preposition \textit{do} ‘\textit{to’}, \REF{ex:tom:plfriday}, and in the presence of \textit{aż} (‘\textit{aż do Piątku’}) the meaning would be that Friday is late, which would be very odd in this context (we saw this effect in \REF{ex:tom:62c}). The temporal \textit{až} in Slovak is reminiscent of the complementizer \textit{aż} in Polish, in that a temporal endpoint is specified without evoking noteworthiness.

\ea\label{ex:tom:sl1}
\ea\label{ex:tom:sl1a} 
\gll V dňoch pondelok až piatok bude predajňa zatvorená.\\
in days Monday \textsc{až} Friday \textsc{aux} store closed\\
\glt `In days from Monday to Friday, the store will be closed.'
\ex\label{ex:tom:sl1b}            
\gll Keby budeš budúci týždeň na služobke? Utorok až piatok.\\
when \textsc{aux} next week on business.trip Tuesday \textsc{až} Friday\\
\glt `When will you be on a business trip next week? Tuesday through Friday.'\\\hfill (Slovak; Andrea Kellö Žačoková, Jana Bašnáková, p.c.)
\z 
\z

\ea\label{ex:tom:plfriday}
\gll W dniach od poniedziałku do piątku sklep będzie zamknięty.\\
in days from Monday to Friday store \textsc{aux} closed\\
\glt `In days from Monday to Friday, the store will be closed.'
\z

\noindent The following example, \REF{ex:tom:sl2}, shows that in Slovak \textit{až} can be used with intervals in general (not just with temporal endpoints as above) and again in Polish in such cases the preposition \textit{do} must be used without \textit{až}, \REF{ex:tom:pl400}, or else the sentence would carry the implication that 400 is significantly more than 384 pages.

\ea\label{ex:tom:sl2}
\gll Naša kniha bude mať 384 až 400 strán.\\
our book \textsc{aux} have 384 \textsc{až} 400 pages\\
\glt `Our book will be 384 to 400 pages long.'\\\hfill (Slovak; Andrea Kellö Žačoková, Jana Bašnáková, p.c.)
\z

\ea\label{ex:tom:pl400}
\gll Nasza książka będzie mieć od 384 do 400 stron.\\
our book \textsc{aux} have from 384 to 400 pages\\
\glt `Our book will be 384 to 400 pages long.'
\z


\noindent The differences in the requirements on the scalar dimension with \textit{aż}/\textit{až} in Polish, Czech and Slovak predict that in different languages we may find exclusives specified for different scales. With temporal scales either an inherent ordering is lexicalized (Czech and Slovak \textit{až}) or the context specifies the direction of the scale (so that the prejacent is either later or earlier than expected, as with Polish \textit{dopiero}). And while for %the 
Polish \textit{dopiero} I opted to assume an extra presupposition to account for temporality, for the Slovak \textit{až} I would consider a meaning without a scalar presupposition but encoding scalarity in the assertion (see \citealt{charnavel15} for French scalar opposites of \textit{even} and \textit{only}).

In South Slavic languages, the adverbial particle \textit{čak} has the same semantics as \textit{aż} (\citealt{tomaszewicz13,tomaszewicz13a}), and in Bulgarian, which lacks a counterpart of \textit{dopiero}, \textit{čak} fulfills this role. Just like in Polish, in \REF{ex:tom:95} \textit{čak} contributes the reading that Mary is a noteworthy person for me to talk to. In \REF{ex:tom:96} \textit{čak} would be translated as \textit{dopiero} rather than \textit{aż} to yield the meaning that ‘now’ is a noteworthy time for you to tell me, because it is late, because as we saw in \sectref{sec:tom:4.3} it is more specific: It calls for a contextually relevant ranking of times.

\ea\label{ex:tom:95}
\gll Govorix čak s Mary.\\            
talked \textsc{čak} with Mary\\
\glt `I talked to somebody so important as Mary.’\\\hfill (Bulgarian; \cite[(4)]{tomaszewicz13}, \cite[(1)]{tomaszewicz13a})
\z 

\ea\label{ex:tom:96}
\gll Zašto mi kazvaš čak sega? \\
why me tell \textsc{čak} now\\
\ea `Why are you telling me only now?’
\ex \textit{Unavailable:} `Why are you telling me even now?’\\\hfill (Bulgarian; \cite[(10)]{tomaszewicz13})
\z 
\z 

\noindent Notably, Bosnian-Croatian-Serbian (BCS) lacks a single lexical item with the meaning of \textit{even} (recall the semantics of \textit{even} in \REF{ex:tom:79}, contrasting it with \textit{aż} in \REF{ex:tom:78}). Instead, the two meaning components of \textit{even}, scalarity and additivity, are lexicalized separately by \textit{čak} and \textit{i}, where \textit{i} is the conjunction \textit{and} (\citealt{jasinskajatomaszewicz16}). In example \REF{ex:tom:97a} \textit{čak} appears alone, while in \REF{ex:tom:97b} it is accompanied by \textit{i}, which contributes additivity absent in \REF{ex:tom:97a}, as evidenced by the continuation. 

\ea\label{ex:tom:97}
\ea\label{ex:tom:97a} 
\gll Poslao sam  žalbu čak  dekanu, ali nisam nikome   drugome.\\
sent \textsc{aux} complaint \textsc{čak} dean but \textsc{neg.aux} nobody other\\
\glt `I sent my complaint to somebody so important as the dean, but I did not send it to anyone else.’
\ex\label{ex:tom:97b}    
\gll Poslao sam žalbu čak i dekanu, \minsp{\#} ali nisam nikome   drugome.\\
sent \textsc{aux} complaint \textsc{čak} and dean {} but \textsc{neg.aux} nobody other\\
\glt `I sent my complaint even to the dean \textit{[Incongruous:]} but I did not send it to anyone else.’\hfill (BCS; \cite[(27)]{jasinskajatomaszewicz16})
\z 
\z 



\noindent In \REF{ex:tom:98a} \textit{čak} together with negation yields the reading ‘low on the scale of distance’, while in \REF{ex:tom:98b} the presence of the additive particle (its negated variant \textit{ni}) yields the ‘low on the scale of likelihood’ reading, which is exactly the reading with \textit{even} in its %the 
English translation.

\ea\label{ex:tom:98}
\ea\label{ex:tom:98a} 
\gll Ivan nije čak u Salzburgu, sad je u Zagrebu.\\     
Ivan \textsc{neg.aux} \textsc{čak} in Salzburg now is in Zagreb\\
\glt `Ivan is not as far as Salzburg, he is in Zagreb now.’
\ex\label{ex:tom:98b}    
\gll Ivan nije čak ni u \minsp{\{} Zagrebu / \minsp{\#} Salzburgu\}.\\
Ivan \textsc{neg.aux} \textsc{čak} \textsc{neg}.and in {} Zagreb {} {} Salzburg\\
\glt `Ivan is not even in Zagreb / \textit{[Incongruous:]} Salzburg.’\\\hfill(BCS; \cite[(28--29)]{jasinskajatomaszewicz16})
\z 
\z 

\noindent The facts from BCS %Bosnian-Croatian-Serbian 
suggest that the different meaning components that I identified for the four Polish exclusives can be lexicalized separately, which adds to the other known cases of compositional combination of particles from the exclusive and additive family, e.g. \textit{auch nur} in German \citep{guerzoni03} (\textit{auch} means `also', \textit{nur} means `only', \textit{auch nur} means `even'), \textit{mégis} in Hungarian (Katja Jasinskaja, p.c.; \citealt{csirmazslade20}) (\textit{még} means `still', \textit{is} is an additive particle and \textit{mégis}  is a scalar additive particle).


The case of BCS \textit{čak i} shows that the repertoire of exclusive and additive scalar particles uses the same building blocks at the levels of assertion and presupposition.\footnote{The importance of the distinction between the asserted and presupposed content in the meaning of \textit{even} and \textit{only} goes back to \citealt{horn69}.} The presupposed and asserted components may also end up being swapped, as argued in \citet{guerzoni03} for subspecies of \textit{even} and \textit{only}, or the strength of particular components may differ, e.g. additivity may be weaker as proposed by \citet{crnic11,crnic12} for concessive additive particles. Moreover, the differences in presuppositions have grammatical effects, i.e., affect the distribution of different particles in different syntactic environments \citep{giannakidou07}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:tom:7}

My analysis of the four Polish exclusives \textit{tylko}, \textit{(za)ledwie}, \textit{dopiero} and \textit{aż} adds to the body of work arguing that we can go beyond the identification of the different flavors of exclusive and additive meanings (as in \citealt{koenig91}) but track the origin of those flavors in their minimal building blocks (\citealt{rullmann97}, \citealt{guerzoni03}, \citealt{lahiri10}, \citealt{crnic11}, \citealt{coppockbeaver11, coppockbeaver10, coppockbeaver14}, a.o.). I identified which meaning components are core and thus reoccur in the different exclusive particles and which components require specific tweaking of parameters. Different settings for specific parameters may influence the flavor of exclusivity, e.g., force the temporal interpretation with \textit{dopiero}, but may also extend their uses beyond that of variants of \textit{only}: I showed that in Polish the restrictions on the alternative set with \textit{(za)ledwie} yield approximative/counterfactual uses, and with \textit{aż} they result in the meaning of \textit{until}.  This work could also be a starting point for the exploration of other expressions with exclusive meaning, such as gradable adjectives, non-focus associating adverbs, etc., for the identification of parameters other than the semantic ingredients that I isolated here. \citet{coppockbeaver11,coppockbeaver10,coppockbeaver14} argue that English adverbial \textit{only} and \textit{just}, and adjectival exclusives \textit{mere}, \textit{sole}, \textit{only}, \textit{single}, \textit{exclusive} vary along two parameters: the ontological type of their arguments and constraints on the question under discussion. \citet{greenberg16,greenberg22} suggests that the variability in the dimensions of the scale could be captured if the scalar presupposition is defined in terms of degrees, with respect to a contextually supplied gradable property. For Polish the next step would be to investigate the building blocks and find a common core with \textit{even}-like particles (\textit{nawet}, which we saw in this chapter, and \textit{choć(by)}), additives (\textit{i}, \textit{też}, \textit{także}, \textit{również}, …), approximatives (the approximative \textit{ledwie} that we saw here belongs to a rich repertoire: \textit{prawie}, \textit{niemal}, \textit{niemalże}, \textit{nieomal}, \textit{omal}, \textit{mało co},…) and exceptives (\textit{oprócz}, \textit{poza}, \textit{ponadto}, \textit{zresztą}, …).
